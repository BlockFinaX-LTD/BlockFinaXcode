In 2022 the Ghanaian cedi went through one of the steepest declines of any currency that year. By November, the US International Trade Administration reported that the cedi had declined by more than 55% between January and October, and that Ghanaian importers were struggling to stay in business because of high import and port clearance costs, with some unable to raise enough money to finance planned shipments [1]. Consumer prices followed. The Ghana Statistical Service recorded year-on-year inflation of 54.1% in December 2022, with food inflation at 59.7% [2]. In May 2023 the IMF approved a three-year arrangement of about $3 billion, listing cedi depreciation alongside falling reserves, rising inflation and collapsing domestic investor confidence among the shocks that had turned into an acute crisis [3].

Those are national figures. This article follows the move to the level where it was felt: an invoice, a margin and a payment date.

## Two ways to describe the same move

Ghana's exchange rate is usually quoted as cedis per US dollar. A move from 7.50 to 11.00 can be described in two ways, and they give different numbers. The dollar's price in cedis has risen by 46.7%. The cedi's value in dollars has fallen by 31.8%, because each cedi now buys 1/11.00 of a dollar instead of 1/7.50. Reports of "depreciation" do not always say which measure they use, which is one reason figures for 2022 vary between sources. For a business paying a dollar invoice, the first measure is the relevant one: it is the increase in the cedis needed to settle the same bill.

## One invoice through the move

The figures that follow are hypothetical and chosen to illustrate a move of the scale seen in 2022. They are not historical market rates.

A pharmaceutical wholesaler in Kumasi orders $300,000 of medicines from a manufacturer in India, payable in 120 days. When it accepts the order, the rate is 7.50, so the invoice is budgeted at 2,250,000 cedis. By the payment date the rate is 11.00 and the same invoice costs 3,300,000 cedis. The additional 1,050,000 cedis is not a pricing decision or an operating loss in the usual sense. It is the cost of having made a commitment in one currency while running the business in another.

What happens next depends on the business. A wholesaler with strong demand and little competition can raise prices, passing the cost to pharmacies and, eventually, patients. One selling into a price-sensitive market absorbs it, and the margin on that shipment may disappear entirely. A school paying for laboratory equipment cannot pass the cost on mid-year at all. An exporter of cashew nuts sees the opposite: its dollar receipts are worth more cedis, although its imported packaging and fuel cost more too.

## What a hedge would have changed

Three choices were available in principle to the wholesaler when it placed the order: do nothing, fix the rate with a forward, or buy protection that pays if the rate rises past a chosen level.

**Doing nothing** leaves the full 1,050,000 cedis with the business.

**A forward** fixes the rate in advance. If a bank had agreed a 120-day forward at, say, 7.95, the invoice would have cost 2,385,000 cedis whatever happened, and the wholesaler would have been protected from almost all of the move. The forward has two catches. The first is access: a forward is a two-way obligation, so banks offer it only to clients with a credit line, which most small and mid-sized importers do not have. The second is that a forward also removes any gain. Had the cedi strengthened instead, the wholesaler would still have paid 7.95.

**Protection with a strike and a limit** is the third route. Suppose the wholesaler buys a limited USD/GHS Cap on $300,000 with a strike of 7.73 (3% above the rate when bought) and a limit of 8.89. A contract of this shape pays the amount protected multiplied by the move from the strike to the settlement rate, up to the limit, divided by the rate when bought. At a settlement rate of 11.00, the move counts only up to 8.89, so the payout is 300,000 × (8.89 − 7.73) ÷ 7.50 = $46,400. Assuming a premium of 1.5% of the amount, $4,500, plus a 5% fee of $225, the protection cost $4,725 upfront. After a 2% fee on the payout, the wholesaler receives $45,472, worth about 500,000 cedis at the new rate.

That covers roughly half of the additional cost, not all of it. The limit did exactly what it was designed to do: it kept the premium low by leaving moves beyond 8.89 uncovered, and 2022 was a year in which the move went far beyond it.

**Open cover** handles a large move differently. Instead of a limit set by the buyer, the protection pays up to a maximum percentage of the amount. With a 30% maximum, the payout limit is $90,000, reached when the rate passes 9.98. At 11.00 the wholesaler receives $88,200 after the payout fee, about 970,000 cedis, covering most of the additional cost. Assuming a premium of 2.5%, the upfront cost would have been $7,875 including the fee, more than limited cover because the protected range is wider.

## What none of these would have changed

Every route above protects a single invoice. None of them changes what the currency's fall did to the rest of the economy. Inflation at 54.1% raised the cedi cost of salaries, rent and transport [2]. Customers' incomes fell in dollar terms, so demand for imported goods weakened even for businesses whose own costs were protected.

Protection also comes with costs of its own. In a calm year, the wholesaler pays premiums and receives nothing, and that is the expected outcome most of the time. A business that hedges every invoice through a stable period will look, on paper, as if it has wasted money. The value only appears in a year like 2022, and nobody knows in advance which year that will be.

Timing matters as well. Protection pays on a reference rate on a specified date, such as the Bank of Ghana's daily weighted median of spot transactions reported by banks [4]. If the wholesaler's payment slips by a month, or its bank sells dollars at a rate well above the official reference, the payout will not match the extra cost exactly.

## Reading 2022 as a planning problem

The useful lesson from 2022 is less about predicting the cedi than about deciding in advance how much of a move a business can survive. A firm that could absorb a 10% rise in the dollar's cedi price but not a 40% rise does not need protection against every movement. It needs protection that starts around the point where the damage becomes serious and reaches far enough to cover the tail. That is a choice about the strike, the limit or maximum, and the amount, made while the currency is calm and protection is cheaper, rather than after the move has started.

For many Ghanaian importers in 2022, none of these choices was available at a price and size they could use. That gap, rather than any shortage of understanding about the risk, is what left businesses carrying the full cost.
