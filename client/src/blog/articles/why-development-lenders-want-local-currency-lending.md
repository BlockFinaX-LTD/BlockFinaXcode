A development bank lends $20 million to a water utility in an East African city. The utility bills households in local currency and repays the loan in dollars. For the first years the arrangement looks sound: tariffs cover operating costs and debt service with room to spare. Then the local currency falls by a third against the dollar. The utility's revenue in local currency has not changed, but each dollar repayment now takes 50% more of it. Tariffs cannot rise that fast without political consequences, so the utility cuts maintenance, delays expansion or falls behind on the loan. The project was viable when it was financed. The currency mismatch made it fragile.

This pattern is common enough that the G20, the Global Infrastructure Facility and the IMF produced a joint report on it in October 2024 [1]. It is also why development lenders talk increasingly about lending in local currency.

## Where the mismatch comes from

The 2024 report identifies three reasons infrastructure in emerging and developing economies ends up exposed. Long-term finance in local currency is scarce, so loans are usually in hard currency. Most infrastructure earns local-currency revenue, from tolls, tariffs and fees, so it has no natural hedge unless it exports. And part of the cost, from imported turbines to foreign engineering, is itself priced in hard currency [1]. The result is that only 26% of private investment in infrastructure in developing markets is made in local currency, and sectors whose revenues are typically in local currency accounted for only 11% of projects with private participation in low- and middle-income countries [1].

Development finance institutions face the same constraint from the other side. TCX, a fund set up in 2007 with development finance institutions such as FMO, KfW, EBRD, EIB and IFC among its shareholders, estimates that more than 80% of lending to low and lower middle-income countries by multilateral development banks and development finance institutions is in hard currency [2]. In small and mid-sized low-income economies, 70 to 85% of debt is denominated in hard currencies [2]. The lender is protected from the currency. The borrower is not.

## Why the borrower is the wrong party to hold the risk

Currency moves in these economies are not rare. TCX's analysis of nearly 100 currencies since 1971 found that, on average, 12.5% of developing-country currencies fell by 20% or more against the US dollar in any given year, and 5% fell by more than half [2]. Over the ten or fifteen years of an infrastructure loan, a large move is close to certain for many borrowers.

A utility or a small business is poorly placed to carry that exposure. It has one currency of revenue, one country of operation, and no portfolio across which losses in one market are offset by gains in another. A diversified investor holding exposure to dozens of currencies is in a very different position, because the currencies do not all fall at once. TCX's proposal puts the aggregate scale of the problem at about $2 trillion of unhedged currency exposure in developing economies, which it expects could triple to $6 trillion by 2030 without action [2].

## What lending in local currency requires

A development bank that lends in Tanzanian shillings or Ghanaian cedis has taken the currency risk onto its own balance sheet, unless it can pass that risk on. There are a few ways to do that. Back-to-back funding means raising money in the same local currency, amount and term as the loan, which works only where a local bond market can supply it. Blended finance uses donor or philanthropic capital to subsidise hedge premiums, guarantee losses or absorb the first tranche of risk [3]. The most direct route is a currency hedge from a provider willing to hold the risk.

TCX exists to be that provider where commercial markets are absent. It reports nearly $20 billion of development loans hedged in 71 currencies since 2007 [4]. Its activity shows both what is possible and how uneven it remains. In 2025 Tanzania was the largest market in Sub-Saharan Africa for the fourth year running, with $159 million hedged [5]. Ghana drew 8 hedges worth $24.2 million and Nigeria 2 hedges worth $7.5 million [5]. The difference reflects demand from lenders, regulation and market conditions in each country as much as TCX's own appetite, but it illustrates how concentrated hedged local-currency lending still is.

Cost is the other limit. The Climate Policy Initiative notes that hedging premiums in emerging and developing economies can reach 6 to 7% of principal, high enough to erase the benefit of borrowing in hard currency [3]. When a hedge costs that much, a borrower can be better off accepting the dollar loan and hoping the currency holds, which returns the risk to where it started.

## The approach the G20 report recommends

The 2024 report frames the answer in two steps: first reduce currency risk, mainly by aligning the currency of revenue with the currency of financing, and then mitigate the part that cannot be removed through financial and non-financial mechanisms [1]. Reducing risk is a long-term project that depends on deeper local bond markets and domestic savings. Mitigating it depends on there being enough capital, somewhere, willing to hold emerging-market currency risk at a price borrowers can pay.

That second step is where the gap is widest. Thin markets mean few counterparties, and few counterparties mean high prices and short terms. TCX's own impact report notes that forward curves in markets without functioning hedging rarely extend beyond six to twelve months [5], far shorter than the life of an infrastructure loan.

## Where smaller exposures fit

Most of the discussion around local-currency finance concerns large loans to utilities, power producers and microfinance institutions. The same mismatch runs through ordinary trade: a distributor paying for imported goods in dollars, a hospital buying equipment, an exporter waiting for payment. These exposures are shorter and smaller, which makes them easier to hedge in principle and harder to serve in practice, because they fall below the size and documentation thresholds of the institutions that hedge development loans. TCX itself relies on an intermediary, MFX, to reach smaller impact funds that cannot meet its collateral and operational requirements [5].

Our own view, which is interpretation rather than finding, is that widening access at this end of the market depends on two things the development-finance experience points to: bringing additional capital to emerging-market currency risk, and offering protection in sizes and terms that do not require a credit relationship. BlockFinaX works on short-dated protection for this segment, with underwriters' capital reserved against each contract when it is sold. It does not replace long-term hedging of development loans, which needs tenors of many years and institutional counterparties.

For development lenders, the case for local-currency lending is already made. What remains is building enough hedging capacity, at enough tenors and prices, for the risk to move from the borrowers least able to carry it to investors who can.
