A finance manager at a medical supplies distributor in Accra knows exactly what her company owes. The next shipment from India is invoiced at $200,000 and due in 90 days. What she does not know is how many cedis that will take. The same question sits on the desk of a feed mill in Kano paying for imported soybean meal, and of a solar developer in Lagos whose panels are priced in dollars while its customers pay in naira.

Textbooks have an answer: hedge it. Buy the dollars forward, or buy an option that pays if the rate moves against you. For most firms in Ghana and Nigeria, that answer stops at the bank counter. This article looks at why.

## How the rate is quoted, and what a move means

Both currencies are usually quoted as local currency per US dollar. A USD/GHS rate of 11.65 means one dollar costs 11.65 cedis. When that number rises, the dollar has become more expensive in cedis, and the cedi has lost value in dollars. The two are not the same percentage. If the rate goes from 10.00 to 12.50, the dollar's cedi price has risen 25%, while each cedi now buys 20% fewer dollars. For an importer, the first figure is the one that matters: it is the increase in the cedis needed to settle the same invoice.

Both central banks publish an official daily reference. The Bank of Ghana publishes a weighted median of spot transactions that banks report before 3:30 pm each working day [8]. The Central Bank of Nigeria publishes the Nigerian Foreign Exchange Market (NFEM) rate, a volume-weighted average that it describes as the official exchange rate for the day [6].

## A deep market for a few currencies

Foreign exchange is the largest financial market in the world, with turnover of about $9.6 trillion a day in April 2025 [1]. Very little of that touches the currencies of smaller economies. TCX, the development-finance fund that specialises in hedging emerging and frontier currencies, notes that the currencies of more than 100 low-income economies together account for less than 0.2% of all currency trading [2].

Thin trading has a direct consequence for anyone who wants to fix a price for the future. TCX reports that where hedging markets do not function well, forward curves rarely extend beyond six to twelve months [3]. Its 2023 proposal goes further: commercial market prices for a ten-year hedge exist for only 11 developing economies, all of them large, and at a three-year horizon a market exists for only around 20 [2]. A business with a two-year supply contract, or a project with a five-year loan, may simply find nobody quoting.

## Why a quote is harder to get than a price

Even where a bank can price a short forward, it has to decide whether it will deal with the client. A forward contract is an obligation in both directions. If the cedi strengthens after the importer buys dollars forward, the importer owes the bank the difference. Banks therefore treat forwards as credit exposure. They want a credit line, signed derivatives documentation and often collateral, and they ration that capacity towards large, established clients.

The scale of this barrier shows up even among professional investors. TCX describes MFX, a separate vehicle, as an access channel for smaller impact funds that cannot meet the collateral and operational thresholds required to deal with TCX directly [3]. If a specialised fund lending to microfinance institutions needs an intermediary to clear those thresholds, a distributor with a $200,000 invoice is unlikely to clear them alone.

Price is the other filter. The Climate Policy Initiative, summarising hedging in emerging and developing economies, notes that premiums can reach 6 to 7% of principal [4]. That figure comes from a review of hedging for long-term project loans, not from short trade payments, so it should not be read as the cost of hedging a 90-day invoice. It does show how quickly the cost of protection can approach the margin a business is trying to protect.

## How small the formal supply is

TCX is the largest dedicated provider in this space. It reports nearly $20 billion of hedged development loans in 71 currencies since 2007 [5]. Its 2025 impact report breaks that activity down by country. In Ghana, TCX wrote 8 hedges worth $24.2 million in 2025. In Nigeria, it wrote 2 hedges worth $7.5 million [3].

Those figures cover one institution and the loans of development lenders, not the forward books of commercial banks, so they are not a measure of all hedging in either country. They are, however, a measured number for the specialist channel built to fill exactly this gap, in two of the region's largest economies. Read alongside the short forward curves and the access thresholds, they suggest that most firms exposed to the dollar in these markets carry the risk themselves.

## What carrying the risk looks like

Consider the distributor's invoice again, using figures chosen for illustration rather than drawn from market data. Suppose the rate is 11.65 cedis per dollar when the order is placed and 13.40 when payment falls due, a rise of about 15% in the dollar's cedi price. The $200,000 invoice was budgeted at 2,330,000 cedis and costs 2,680,000. The extra 350,000 cedis comes straight out of gross margin, unless the company can raise its prices to customers who are themselves facing higher living costs.

Firms adapt in ways that do not appear in any hedging statistic. Some price their goods with a buffer that assumes the worst, which makes them less competitive when the currency is calm. Some buy dollars early and hold them, which ties up working capital that could fund stock. Others shorten supplier terms or pass the risk to customers by quoting in dollars, where the law and their market allow it. Each of these is a form of self-insurance, and each has a cost that falls on the business rather than on a counterparty paid to carry it.

Policy changes can widen the gap overnight. When Nigeria unified its official exchange-rate windows in June 2023, the naira depreciated sharply, as the IMF later noted in its review of the economy [7]. Unification was intended to make the official rate reflect the market. For a firm that had priced contracts on the old official rate, the move arrived as a single, large step that no short-dated hedge would have covered in full.

## What would widen access

The constraints point to their own remedies. Smaller contract sizes would bring hedging within reach of the firms that need it. Protection that is paid for upfront, rather than settled as a two-way obligation, removes the need for a credit line, because the buyer can never owe more than the premium. Capital that is set aside in full when protection is sold answers the counterparty question directly: the payout exists before the contract does. And pricing that is visible before purchase lets a business compare the cost of protection with the cost of carrying the risk.

BlockFinaX is built around those features. Businesses buy protection on the US dollar against currencies including the cedi and the naira, in amounts as small as a single invoice, for end dates up to 90 days ahead. The premium is paid once in USDC, and the most each contract can pay is reserved from underwriters' capital when it is sold. That does not remove every risk: protection pays on a reference rate rather than the rate a firm actually obtains, and only between the strike and the cap the buyer chooses. It does address the two barriers that keep most firms out, the credit relationship and the minimum size.

The deeper constraint is the one the numbers describe. A currency that trades thinly will always be harder and more expensive to hedge than the dollar or the euro. Widening access is less about inventing new instruments than about bringing more capital to the risk, in sizes and terms that the businesses carrying it can use.
