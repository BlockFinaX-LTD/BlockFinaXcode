What is a liquidity provider here?

You are an underwriter of currency risk. You provide capital that protects African businesses against exchange-rate movement. You collect premium upfront. You pay out only if the rate moves outside the agreed range.

This is insurance underwriting, not lending and not currency speculation. You're not betting on direction — you're being paid for accepting volatility risk within bounded limits.

1
You commit capital

Deposit any amount. It sits in the pool ready to back protection events. The capital is yours — locked, but not spent.

2
Hedgers buy protection

An importer paying $50K in 30 days wants protection against the cedi weakening. They pay you a premium upfront for that protection.

3
Wait 30 days

If the rate stays inside the agreed range, the event expires. You keep the premium. Capital is freed for the next event.

4
If rate moves too far

You pay out — bounded by the cap you set. Your maximum loss per event is fixed in advance. Capital then recycles.

Your return = premium income − payouts you made. The tool below shows what that would have been for any historical period you pick, with the terms you set.
Key assumption — protocol leverage: $1 of LP capital backs $10 of notional protection at any moment (10x leverage). Premium is charged as a % of notional, not as a % of LP capital. This is how the math compounds: you collect premium on $10 of exposure for every $1 of capital you've committed. Hedgers on the platform typically have trade positions roughly 10x their own working capital — an importer with $5K of cash often manages $50K of monthly purchase orders. The protocol leverage matches this market reality.

The leverage multiplies your premium income — but it also multiplies your exposure when events trigger.
How far the rate must move before protection triggers. At today's rate, your range creates a band: rate × (1 − range%) on the bottom, rate × (1 + range%) on top. If the rate stays inside the band during the event, nothing happens — you keep the premium. If the rate exits the band, protection triggers and you pay out (capped). If today's rate is 11.00 and your range is 8%, the boundaries are 10.12 (lower) and 11.88 (upper). The rate has to leave that band for a trigger to fire.
The maximum you pay out if a single event triggers. A 4.5% cap means even in a 30% currency crash, you only pay 4.5% of the notional protected. This bounds your downside per event — unlike selling naked options, your loss can never blow past the cap. On a $50K hedge with a 4.5% cap, your maximum loss if the event triggers is $2,250. The cap should generally be lower than your range width — a 4.5% cap on an 8% range is typical.
What fraction of your capital is actively backing events at any moment. A real LP position is rarely 100% deployed — some capital sits idle as reserve, and matching hedger demand to LP supply is imperfect. 65% is a realistic working target. If you commit $250K at 65% utilization, ~$162K is backing live events at any moment and ~$88K is idle reserve. Year 1 utilization on a new platform is usually lower (30–50%).
How long each protection event lasts, in days. 30 days is typical for SME invoice payments. Shorter tenors mean capital recycles faster (more events per year), but each event's premium is smaller. Longer tenors lock capital longer. A 30-day tenor means $1 of capital backs ~12 sequential events over a year. A 60-day tenor means ~6 events.

What does an LP actually do?

A liquidity provider on BlockFinaX is an underwriter of FX risk. When a Ghanaian importer wants protection against the cedi weakening over the next 30 days, the LP is the counterpart who pays out if the rate moves and keeps the premium if it doesn't.

This is fundamentally insurance underwriting, not lending. You're not loaning capital and earning interest. You're posting capital as collateral against a specific risk and earning premium for accepting that risk. The capital is locked while the event is active. When the event expires, the capital returns to you plus or minus the outcome.

Three things make this distinct from traditional FX trading or DeFi yield farming:

  1. You are not betting on direction. You don't need a view on whether the cedi will strengthen or weaken. You need a view on whether your premium properly compensates for the volatility.
  2. Your loss is bounded. The cap you set limits maximum payout per event. You cannot lose more than that on any single contract — unlike selling naked options, where losses can be unbounded.
  3. Capital recycles. When an event expires, capital is freed for the next one. Over a year, $1 of LP capital backs many sequential events, multiplying premium income.

How to price the premium

Pricing is the core skill of an underwriter. There are three building blocks. Get these right and the math works; get any of them wrong and you lose money to better-priced competitors or to volatility you didn't expect.

1 Estimate the probability of trigger

If the rate has to move 8% in 30 days for the event to trigger, how often does that happen? The honest answer requires looking at historical data for the specific currency, in the specific volatility regime.

Example: USD/GHS
Annualized volatility around 18%. Scaled to 30 days: 18% × √(30/365) ≈ 5.2%. A move of 8% (the range width) is roughly 1.5 standard deviations. Under a normal distribution that's roughly 13% probability — but EM currencies have fat tails, so the real probability is higher, perhaps 18–25%.

2 Calculate fair value

Fair value is the expected payout — the probability of trigger times the cap.

Fair value = P(trigger) × Cap %
Example continued
With 20% trigger probability and a 4.5% cap, fair value is 20% × 4.5% = 0.9% of notional. That is the breakeven premium — at this price, on average, the LP earns nothing.

3 Add the risk premium

The LP cannot accept the breakeven premium because volatility can spike, fat-tail events occur, and regime breaks happen. The risk premium compensates for this — typically 40% to 80% above fair value in EM markets.

Premium = Fair value × (1 + risk multiplier)
Example continued
Fair value of 0.9%, with a 60% risk multiplier, gives a premium of 0.9% × 1.6 = 1.44% of notional. On a $50,000 hedge, that's $720 charged to the hedger.

The risk multiplier is where underwriters compete. Higher multipliers mean higher returns when triggers are rare but reduce the volume of business won. Lower multipliers attract more flow but compress margins and concentrate risk.

Why EM currencies trend toward depreciation

An underwriter who treats USD/EM pairs as random walks gets systematically punished. EM currencies depreciate over time because of structural forces that operate continuously:

  • Inflation differentials. When local inflation is 25% and US inflation is 3%, the local currency loses about 22% of its purchasing power per year.
  • Productivity gaps. Countries whose output growth lags developed economies see their currencies weaken over time.
  • Capital flight in stress. Whenever conditions deteriorate, savers move money to dollars.
  • Debt service in foreign currency. Many EM economies borrow in dollars. Servicing that debt creates structural demand for USD.
  • Pegs and managed floats break. When a central bank defends an overvalued rate, the eventual devaluation is concentrated rather than gradual.

The practical implication: pricing that assumes the rate moves both ways with equal probability is wrong. The rate has a drift, and the drift is one direction.

Probability is not destiny — regime matters

The probability of trigger isn't a single number. It depends on the regime the currency is in. The same range width that triggers 10% of the time in a calm period might trigger 50% of the time in a crisis.

USD/GHS regime comparison
2020–2021 (calm): Cedi moved less than 5% over the entire 24-month period. An 8% range almost never triggered. An underwriter charging 1% premium made money easily.

2022–2023 (crisis): Cedi went from 6 to 12. An 8% range triggered almost every month. An underwriter still charging 1% premium got destroyed. The honest premium for the new regime was 4–6%.

This is why the backtest tab matters. Don't pick a single regime and assume it represents the future. Test your terms across multiple regimes — calm periods, crisis periods, reversal periods — and only commit capital if the strategy survives in all of them.

The questions you should ask before committing capital

How is BlockFinaX going to source enough hedger demand? ▾
Distribution is the gating factor. The premium-and-loss math means nothing if there's no hedger paying the premium. Before committing LP capital, you should see the customer pipeline: which Ghanaian importer/exporter networks are committed to using the platform, what their estimated monthly hedge volume is, and what conversion rate they've achieved in pilots.
What is the smart contract risk and how is it bounded? ▾
Smart contracts can fail in ways that lose LP capital — oracle manipulation, code exploits, governance attacks. Mitigations: multiple independent audits, time-locked governance changes, maximum exposure per event. A sophisticated LP haircuts modelled returns by 1–2% to account for unquantified contract risk.
What happens during regime breaks the model doesn't capture? ▾
Step devaluations like Egypt 2024 (35% in one week) or Ethiopia 2024 (more than 100% in 6 months) violate the assumptions of normal-distribution pricing. An LP exposed to a single such event with naive pricing can lose multiple years of premium income in a single month.
How does adverse selection work in this market? ▾
Hedgers buy protection when they expect the rate to move against them. They may know more about specific shipping cycles, supplier payment timing, or local political risk than the underwriter does. Compensating for this requires adding 10–20% to the risk premium beyond what pure historical volatility implies.
What is the lockup, and what is my real-world liquidity? ▾
Capital is locked for the period you commit to. There is no in-period exit unless a secondary market exists (it doesn't yet). If you need optionality on your capital, the LP position is not suitable.

How to use this reference

Before underwriting a new currency, check three things:

  1. Regime stability. Has the central bank held the rate stable for years, or are there regular step-devaluations? Step-devaluations require wider ranges and higher caps than smooth movement.
  2. Recent move size. What was the largest 30-day or 90-day move in the past 3 years? Your range width should be larger than this.
  3. Inflation and policy. Inflation above 15% and a central bank rate above 20% are signals that the currency is under depreciation pressure.
